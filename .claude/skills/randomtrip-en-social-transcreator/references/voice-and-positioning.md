# Positioning and voice — translation reference

## Source-derived foundations
From `RandomTrip_New Final_Brand_Positioning_v2.docx`, `RandomTrip_Brand_Manifesto.docx`, `Brand Principles.txt` and `Randomtrip Brand Book 3.1 ESP.pdf`.

### Why Randomtrip exists
Travel has become over-researched, overplanned and overoptimized. People spend too long comparing destinations, reviews, hotels, tabs, lists and itineraries, especially for short escapes. Randomtrip creates room for possibility by removing unnecessary choices and providing carefully curated trips.

- **Purpose:** Make travel feel magical again by helping people find trips they would never have chosen but end up loving.
- **Vision:** More curiosity, fewer assumptions.
- **Belief:** The best experiences are not always the ones we choose.
- **Territory:** Discovery Beyond Choice.
- **Essence:** Serendipity by Design.
- **Promise:** Trips you wouldn't have chosen—but end up loving.
- **North Star:** Wonder begins where certainty ends.
- **Values:** Curiosity, trust and ease.

### Critical distinction
The destination being unknown is not itself the consumer promise. That is how the service removes certainty and habitual choice. The reward is to discover a place, experience, encounter or story that otherwise might never have entered the traveler's world. The result **feels spontaneous**, even when the experience is thoughtfully assembled.

Don't frame the company primarily as:
- A mystery game or competition to guess a destination.
- Algorithmic matching / personalization as the whole point.
- A standard agency that saves time and guarantees the “perfect” trip.
- A destination checklist or a catalogue of famous landmarks.
- A platform that “randomly” assigns a place.

### Two consumer tensions, two different jobs
1. **Discover the unexpected:** desire, long-term brand differentiation, stories and emotion. Make readers feel curious about what they haven't thought to choose.
2. **Planning fatigue:** relevancy, identification with too many decisions, a clean entry to practical offers and product understanding. Humor about open tabs, star ratings and contradictory reviews works well.

A piece has a primary tension. It can have a secondary reinforcement, but avoid overcrowding its hero line/visual with two separate slogans.

### Four social content pillars (project decisions)
- **Desire and Discovery:** emotional brand, experiences and stories that weren't on your radar.
- **Problem and Proposition:** planning fatigue, then how the offer works and what it solves.
- **Anticipation / Expectation:** different ways to live Randomtrip, real past moments, contexts and styles.
- **Trust:** address objections, transparency and the people doing the curating.

These pillars are strategic routing labels, **not copy that should be placed automatically in a caption**.

## Voice → everyday English

| Source brand trait | English in practice | Not this |
|---|---|---|
| Curious | “What if the next place you love isn't one you'd ever search for?” | “Explore an extensive array of exciting destinations.” |
| Human | “Pack a bag. We'll take it from here.” | “Our innovative platform provides tailored itineraries.” |
| Light | Short, direct, conversational; breath between thoughts | Every caption is a manifesto, poetic in every line |
| Unexpected | “You know the date. The destination? Not yet.” | “Unveil the thrill of an exciting mystery escape!” |
| Reassuring | “Unexpected doesn't mean left to chance.” | “A random algorithm will choose for you.” |
| Challenger | “Thirty tabs open. Still no trip booked?” | Mocking the customer for being careful |

### Humor and provocation
- The target of the joke is **choice overload**, repetitive tourist habits and the promise of absolute control — not vulnerable people, fear or legitimate consumer objections.
- A short reply may be cheeky. A complaint or a genuine practical question should be helpful before it is cheeky.
- Avoid hard-sell, aggressive trolling, sarcasm that reads as contempt, or manufactured memes that drown out the insight.

### Collection of Moments
- Tell stories that **actually happened** in the past tense, if original is a real experience. Do not invent UGC, quotes, participants, encounters or emotional reactions.
- A country can be context (`This time, it was Brazil.`), not the whole payoff. The story is about what was discovered beyond habitual choices.
- Only state worldwide departures when the source explicitly says so or the relevant offer facts support it.

### Photography and design notes (when translator is responsible for artwork text)
- Editorial / filmic / collected-not-produced rather than shiny tourism adverts.
- Authentic, imperfect, sometimes intimate everyday moments, not mandatory scenic postcard shots.
- Main headline: uppercase Barlow Condensed Medium/Bold; explanatory text: Barlow Regular; handwriting accent: Nothing You Could Do used sparingly.
- Use left/right text alignment unless center alignment is used intentionally to emphasize; maintain breathing room.
- Brand Book palette: Off White `#F3F2ED`, Mineral Sage `#A4B4AA`, Deep Teal `#0F5C60`, Ochre `#E5A51C` (accent). Colors are visual guidance, **not a translation instruction**.
- If working only with text, **never claim** to have changed the art or photography.

### Canonical brand-name handling
Use `Randomtrip` in normal copy per social brand convention. Preserve `RANDOMTRIP` in all-caps branded lockups when already rendered. Do not create a replacement logotype or manually rebuild the compass/die icon; respect the approved asset.
